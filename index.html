What the files cannot tell me
  • No deal_id on a search row — I know how many results appeared, never which ones, and never what was actually bought.
  • No user_id or session — a customer who searched, got nothing, retyped, and then bought looks like two unrelated rows.
  • No position or ranking, no time of day, no coordinates.
  • No currency field — prices are labeled USD across five non-US markets.
  • No relevance label — I had to define “a correct match” myself. Validated it three independent ways, but it’s still a judgment call, not a measurement.

That’s why some of what follows is a predictive signal, not proof. With no ID chain from one search to one outcome, a pattern this consistent is strong evidence — it isn’t the same as tracing one customer’s actual journey.

Part A · What is broken

Search returns results that have nothing to do with what the catalog holds.

I found fifteen findings — fifteen separate things measured in the two CSVs. They are not fifteen pieces of work. Fourteen of them group into three problems, and the fifteenth is the scoreboard the other fourteen are judged against. One build fixes the first two problems. The third cannot be fixed by search at all.

Conversion is a step, not a slope

Finding 01 is not one of the three problems — nothing about it can be built. It is how you know the three problems cost money, and it sets exactly one goal for whatever is built next. It carries no score for the same reason.

Conversion does not decline gently as results get thinner. It falls off a cliff between three results and two. Searches returning one or two results convert at 1.7%; searches returning four to twenty-five convert at 17.8% — ten times better.

29.3% of searches return nothing, and every dashboard watches that number. The 23.2% that return one or two results are watched by nobody, because they are not zero.

Healthy band Failing band

Bar height is purchase rate. Every search in the month, grouped by how many results it returned.

andmore choice is not the goal

The obvious reading — people buy when there is more to choose from — is wrong, and the same data kills it. Split the healthy band into quarters and conversion does not rise with choice. It drifts down.

Four results convert better than twenty-five. It is a threshold, not a slope: below three you are dead, above twenty-five you are dead, and anywhere in between behaves much the same.

So the goal is to land in the band, never to fill it. Padding a result set to look fuller is the one thing this number rules out — and it is exactly what a system that invents its own count does by accident.

The count is invented

The number of results a search returns is produced without looking at the catalog. That single defect shows up four ways — as no correlation with the catalog, as counts that cannot exist, as an answer that changes on every run, and as pages of forty. Search fixes all four, and one change fixes them together.

02Blind to the catalog

Manchester’s entire catalog is nineteen deals. A search for gym there returned thirty-five. That is not a ranking problem or a relevance problem — the result count is produced without ever looking at what the city holds.

585 searches — 6.5% of the month — returned more results than their city holds deals in total. Nothing downstream could have caught it, because nothing downstream compares the two numbers.

Across the whole month the two are unrelated. For every search I counted how many deals in that city genuinely match what the customer asked for, then compared it with how many results the system returned. If retrieval worked, the two would rise together. They do not move together at all: r = +0.02.

That number depends on my definition of “matching”, so I tested three. My own intent map gives +0.02. The catalog’s own five categories give +0.01. No map at all — just every deal in the city — gives +0.02. The finding is not my judgment. It is in the data.

Findings 02 and 07 in the table below are the same defect at two altitudes — the correlation is the measurement, the impossible counts are the existence proof — so they are shown together here.

03Unstable results

The catalog is a single monthly snapshot. It does not change. Yet a repeated query in a fixed city swings from zero results to forty across thirty days.

Of the 411 query-and-city pairs searched at least eight times, 337 ever return anything. Not one of those 337 returns a stable count. 80.1% of them hit both zero and twenty-plus in the same month. The typical spread is 31 results.

The words don’t match

Matching happens on the string the customer typed, against titles written by someone else, in another language, in package words rather than product words. Search fixes most of this; retitling the catalog is merchandising’s half.

04Wrong language

A customer in Berlin searching Gesichtsbehandlung gets a median of eight results and buys 10.4% of the time. The same intent, in the same city, typed as facial, gets a median of one.

A query counts as foreign only when that same market also searches that same intent in its own language, and the comparison runs inside those pairs — that leaves 55 market-and-intent cells. The foreign version is worse in 54 of them. 266 foreign-language searches produced two purchases; the 2,819 local-language searches for the same intents produced 293.

05Vocabulary gap

Customers search for 128 distinct things. The catalog has 75 titles, and names them things nobody types: Full Body Massage Package, Tasting Menu for Two, Unlimited Fitness Classes.

Those three generic families — massage, dining, classes — are 256 of the 568 deals. 45% of everything in the catalog, described in words no customer has ever searched.

There is nothing to sell

No retrieval change fills a catalog. This is the quarter of the month that no version of search can answer — and the reason average order value outside the US runs at roughly 60% of US levels.

06Nothing to sell

2,503 searches — 27.8% of the month — ask for one of nine things the catalog does not hold in any of the twenty cities. Not thin supply. None.

Six of them are high-ticket: skydiving, helicopter tours, hot-air balloons, rafting, supercar track days, shark diving. 1,638 searches, and the largest number of results any of them ever returned is zero. Zero clicks. Zero revenue. Every time.

Three are ordinary-priced, and they are worse: paintball, bowling and eyelash extensions. 865 searches — and unlike the six, these returned up to forty results, took 300 clicks and ended in 83 purchases. Against a catalog that does not hold them. Whatever those 83 people bought, it was not what they searched for, and no dashboard has ever flagged it.

And the ceiling underneath all of it: the most expensive deal in the entire catalog costs $179.46. The median is $92. A customer who arrived ready to spend €400 has nothing to buy.

1,638 = the 1,512 rows labeled no-inventory intent in searches_classified.csv plus 126 misspellings of the same six concepts (shark diiving, helicopeer tour). They are 62.2% of the month’s 2,633 zero-result searches.

Part B · The prototype

Honest Search.

A working search box on this page, running on the real 568-deal catalog and the real 8,997-row log. Type anything into it. Every answer is rendered twice — OLD, replaying what the log actually recorded for that query in that city, and NEW, what a search that reads intent, checks the city’s real catalog and says what it just did would return. It is a working prototype with the explanations built around it, not a finished interface for an end user.

01

Pick a kind of query, or type your own. Eleven chips, grouped under the same three problems as Part A. Whatever you type, the chip for its kind lights up — so a typed query always says which of the three it belongs to.

02

Read the line between the panes. One of eight answers, always: matched, translated, corrected, category only, thin, not sold here, above ceiling, not understood. The NEW pane opens by naming what changed against OLD.

03

Change the city — or pick “All markets”. Twenty cities with their real deal counts. The same query answers differently in London and in Kraków; that is the point, not a bug. “All markets” checks the whole catalog in one step.

04

Open “Show more information”. The Part A finding behind that answer, what today does, what this does instead, whether search can fix it at all — and proof that every one of the 8,997 logged searches gets one of the eight answers.

Part C · The writeup

Two pages, four points.

01

What I found, with the numbers

Part A carries the evidence. This is its shape: fifteen findings, one scoreboard, and three problems underneath it.

 FindingsWhat the data says
The scoreboard01 Conversion by result count: 0 → 0%, 1–2 → 1.7%, 4–25 → 17.8%, 26–40 → 2.3%. Only 42.3% of the month lands in the band that sells. A step, not a slope.

The words don’t match04 05 09 10 Titles are in the local language, so 347 foreign-language searches converted at 0.58% against 10.58%. Madrid holds zero deals naming sushi; its 44 sushi searches got generic dining menus.
There is nothing to sell06 11 12 13 Nine concepts missing from every catalog in five markets: 2,503 searches, 27.8% of the month. The most expensive deal anywhere is $179.46 — the AOV gap, as a number.

The split that decides what to build. Of the 2,633 searches that returned nothing, 1,121 (43%) are search’s to fix — the city holds a matching deal and search missed it. The other 1,512 are not, because no retrieval change creates a helicopter operator.

02

What I decided to build, and why that rather than the other options

One engine, for all fifteen findings. I scored each finding by likely value to get the build order, but the build is one thing rather than a queue of fifteen: retrieval that reads intent, checks what the city holds, and says plainly when the answer is nothing. It fixes nine outright; finding 01 is the scoreboard it is judged against.

The other five it cannot fix, so it answers them honestly. Findings 06, 11, 12 and 13 are supply and merchandising — no search change fills a catalog. The prototype does the three things still available: says outright that the city doesn’t sell it, offers one nearby concept labeled a different experience, not a replacement, and captures the demand with a notify-me. A supply lead with a number on it, instead of a dead end.

What I chose not to buildWhy not
An LLM over the catalog Not never — not first. The count doesn’t match the catalog, and a language model over a broken count only makes the same wrong answer fluent. It also re-creates finding 03: answering differently every time is what a probabilistic layer does by design. Fix the retrieval first — see AI-driven personalization, below.
A “similar deals” rail on the empty state What the live site does today: an unlabeled grid the customer is invited to read as relevant. Same defect as everything above, wearing a helpful face.
Personalization and recommendations No user_id and no session in the data. Building it would mean inventing the signal it runs on.
A learned ranking model No result position and no deal_id to train or evaluate against. And the money is not there: inside the band, 4–9 results convert at 19.8% and 20–25 at 16.9%. Landing in the band beats ordering within it.

AI-driven personalization. The model is not the hard part — the data underneath it is. What someone bought before, what they searched, what the merchant knows: none of it is in this file, which is the same gap that rules out personalization above. With it, this stops being a prompt and becomes a model fine-tuned on our own customers. It brings its own weight — evals and safety checks, a customer-context store to serve it from, the AI-ops to keep it honest in production. Which is why it is layers, not either/or: deterministic retrieval is the floor that makes the count true, and the model sits on top of it once there is something to learn from and a way to prove it helps.

03

What I’d do next with an engineering team, and what I’d need from the platform teams who own search globally

The first move is not a ticket. I can prove the number coming back doesn’t match the city’s catalog. I can’t tell you why — whether the index holds the catalog and applies it wrongly, or never reads it, isn’t visible in a search log. So: take the finding and the prototype to the team that owns retrieval, architect in the room, and have them tell me where the count comes from. The spec gets written there, jointly: my half is the behavior and the acceptance tests, theirs is the mechanism. A PM writing someone else’s architecture from a CSV is how you get a spec nobody builds.

Then delivery, in three slices. Each ships on its own and carries a number from Part A, so any of them can be cut if the estimate comes back heavy:

From catalog and merchandising: retitle the repeating catalog, and source the nine missing concepts — 2,503 searches a month, and a $179.46 ceiling that is the AOV gap.

There is no revenue field in the data. That last row is purchase count × the median deal price in the buyer’s own city ($92.34 a purchase), so it moves only when purchases move — it is a translation of the row above it into money, not a second piece of evidence.

Where the targets come from. The first three aren’t guesses — they are what the engine returns when all 8,997 logged searches are re-answered against the real catalog, so the code can be held to them. The last two are the honest ones. The ceiling — +805 purchases, ~$74,332 a month (95% CI $69,556–$80,175) — assumes every recoverable search converts at the healthy-band rate. It is a ceiling, not a forecast, and its interval covers sampling noise only, not that assumption. The bar I’d actually act on is the smallest move this traffic can prove: +10% relative. If the thesis is right it should clear that easily, and the gap between the two is the margin I’m betting on.

When I’d look. Day 3 and week 1, the mechanism gate — if the band hasn’t moved, stop; the code isn’t doing what it claims. Month 1, directional only, not yet powered. Month 4, the powered read and the decision. A/B randomized on the customer rather than the search, rolled out GB first — the change alters the answer for 79.7% of searches, so there is no small blast radius to hide behind. And rising click-through is not success: the 26–40 band already earns clicks at 2.3% conversion, so fewer, truer results should cost clicks.

Success — more searches land in the band that sells, and purchases per addressable search rise by at least 10% relative, about +$6,700 a month. Anything smaller I cannot prove at this traffic, so I will not claim it.

Failure — the band moves exactly as predicted and purchases don’t. That would mean the band was a property of the data rather than of customers, and my whole reading of Part A was wrong.

Row three above is neither. If the band doesn’t move, nothing was tested — that is a build that didn’t ship what it claimed, not a verdict on the idea.

The failure I am guarding against is not “it didn’t work”. It is “it didn’t work and I couldn’t tell you why” — which is why the instrumentation ask comes ahead of any feature.
